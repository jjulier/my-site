Each idea is a section: a `## [Name](Wikipedia link)` heading, then its group, then a quote from the Wikipedia page as a `>` block. Highlight the part to show on the poster with ==double equals==. Anything after the quote is your note about it.

## [Convergent evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_evolution)
Design

> Convergent evolution is ==the independent evolution of similar features in species of different lineages==.

An analogy for how, in an ideal world, design patterns emerge.

## [Hobson's choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hobson%27s_choice)
Design

> A Hobson's choice is ==a free choice in which only one thing is actually offered==. The term is often used to describe an illusion that choices are available.

See also [choice architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choice_architecture) and also the [overton window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overton_window). Always good to think about what choices are off the table and why.

## [Diminishing returns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diminishing_returns)
Design

> if a factor of production continues to increase—while holding all other production factors constant—==at some point, a further incremental unit of input will return a lower amount of output.==

The concept that predates the graph that shows the tipping point where adding more features to you product starts making it worse.

## [Overfitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfitting)
Design

> In mathematical modeling, overfitting is the production of an analysis that ==corresponds too closely or exactly to a particular set of data and may therefore fail to fit to additional data== or predict future observations reliably.

One of the tensions in design too. Build so it meets one audiences needs perfectly, and end up excluding others with a similar need.

## [McNamara fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McNamara_fallacy)
Product

> The first step is to measure whatever can be easily measured. This is okay as far as it goes. The second step is to disregard that which can't be easily measured or give it an arbitrary quantitative value. This is artificial and misleading. The third step is to presume that ==what can't be measured easily really isn't very important==. This is blindness.

Why OKRs and A/B testing on its own doesn't lead to great products people love.

## [All models are wrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_models_are_wrong)
Product

> "All models are wrong" is a common aphorism in statistics, and science more generally. It is often expanded as "==All models are wrong, but some are useful==".

Aka the map is not the territory. One of my least favourite forms of bike shedding is spending too longer trying to make the map or model some sort of simulacra of reality, it's not possible.

## [Sunk cost fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunk_cost)
Product

> In other words, a sunk cost is ==a sum paid in the past that should no longer be relevant to decisions about the future==.

See also the [endowment effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endowment_effect). Basically a reason to ask the question 'Are we spending time on this because we actually still believe it's the right thing to do, or are we just in so deep we can't bear to stop, and thus crystallise the loss?'

## [Opportunity cost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opportunity_cost)
Product

> In microeconomic theory, the opportunity cost of a choice is ==the value of the best alternative forgone== where, given limited resources, a choice needs to be made between several mutually exclusive alternatives.

What are you not doing as a result of focusing on your current thing? Don't just think about the value of the current thing, but also about the value of the stuff that it displaces or pushes later down the line.

## [Streisand effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streisand_effect)
In life

> The Streisand effect is the phenomenon in which ==an attempt to hide, remove, or censor information== results in the unintended consequence of the effort instead ==increasing public awareness of the information==.

In setting out to achieve one outcome, you end up achieving the exact opposite. Often true of overly strict security policies that push people into workarounds.

## [Replication crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_crisis)
In life

> The replication crisis, also known as the reproducibility crisis or replicability crisis, is a widespread phenomenon in which ==published scientific results cannot be reproduced==.

Basically, science isn't always as sciencey as it claims to be.

## [Dramaturgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramaturgy_(sociology))
In life

> The fundamental difference between Burke's and Goffman's view, however, is that ==Burke believed that life was in fact theatre, whereas Goffman viewed theatre as a metaphor.==

One way of thinking about how technology helps change things. Technology is part of the context people operate in. People play different roles in different contexts. Ergo by giving people a different context you can enable them to play a different role.

## [Banal nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banal_nationalism)
In organisations

> Banal nationalism refers to ==everyday representations of a nation, which build a sense of shared national identity==. Examples include sports nationalism, the use of flags or national anthems in everyday contexts, and gastronationalism.

Feelings of belonging or connection to a country (or maybe even a smaller community) come from small banal things, like seeing your country on a weather map, or eating baked beans.

## [Polite fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polite_fiction)
In organisations

> A polite fiction is a social scenario in which ==all participants are aware of a truth, but pretend to believe in some alternative version of events== to avoid conflict or embarrassment.

Where everyone plays along with a fiction they all know isn't really true.

## [Law of triviality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_triviality)
In organisations

> The law of triviality is C. Northcote Parkinson's 1957 argument that ==people within an organization commonly give disproportionate weight to trivial issues==.

Aka 'Bike-shedding' spend time on trivial things to avoid talking about the big scary stuff

## [Bystander effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bystander_effect)
In organisations

> The bystander effect (also called bystander apathy or the Genovese effect) is a social psychological theory that states that ==individuals are less likely to offer help to a victim in the presence of other people==.

An extreme example of the kind of paralysis that happens when accountability isn't clear

## [Argument from authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_from_authority)
In organisations

> An argument from authority (Latin: argumentum ab auctoritate, also called an appeal to authority, or argumentum ad verecundiam) is a form of argument in which ==the opinion of an authority figure (or figures) is used as evidence to support an argument==.

When someone tells you we have to do something because it's in a plan, strategy or because someone important said we have to, instead of because it actually makes sense.

## [Law of the instrument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_instrument)
In organisations

> Abraham Maslow wrote in 1966, "==it is tempting, if the only tool you have is a hammer, to treat everything as if it were a nail.=="

Aka 'If all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail'. This manifests in the way organisation try to apply the latest technology to any problem. It was apps, now it's AI.

## [Mode effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_effect)
In research

> Mode effect is a broad term referring to a phenomenon where ==a particular survey administration mode causes different data to be collected==.

The way you collect data, eg over the phone, online, in person, changes the data you collect.

## [Curse of knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_knowledge)
In research

> The curse of knowledge, also called the curse of expertise or expert's curse, is a cognitive bias that occurs when ==a person who has specialized knowledge assumes that others share in that knowledge==.

Why user research is useful

## [Sampling bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampling_bias)
In research

> If this is not accounted for, ==results can be erroneously attributed to the phenomenon under study rather than to the method of sampling==.

For years I worried about sample size when I should have been worrying about sampling bias. Even if your sample is big enough, if you haven't sampled randomly your results aren't representative.

## [Simpson's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simpson%27s_paradox)
In research

> Simpson's paradox is a phenomenon in probability and statistics in which ==a trend appears in several groups of data but disappears or reverses when the groups are combined==.

Looking at high level aggregate data obscures or reverses important underlying trends and nuance. Classic central gov problem.
